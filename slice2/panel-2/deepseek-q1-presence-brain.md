# Question 1 — Presence Brain

## (a) Failure taxonomy — small model, live text ledger

Distinct failure modes, ordered roughly by how often they will bite in this house:

1. **Wrong-line selection (the observed failure).** The brain surfaces a *true* ledger line that is not the one the question was about — shelf line instead of camera verdict. Not hallucination; a retrieval/ranking failure. The 1.2B model is doing its own retrieval with no discipline, and "shelf" and "camera" both score as "recent, relevant, house state."
2. **Stale-line selection.** It cites a verdict from a previous tick because the ledger contains history and the model has no notion of "latest for this stream." The substrate's own publish rule ("a verdict is a claim about *this* tick") is not visible to a free-reading model.
3. **Confident paraphrase drift.** It reads a correct line and speaks a claim the line does not support — "someone is at the door" from a tile-diff verdict that says "person-scale change, NE tiles, unperceived." The line is honest; the sentence is not.
4. **Silence-as-answer.** Asked "what changed at the dock?", it answers "nothing" because it retrieved no line — when in fact the stream is *stale* or *reflex-only* or *sip-budget* and the honest answer is "I'm not watching that closely right now." This is the Amendment-2 boundary colliding with §3.3's "a watch that admits its blind spots keeps trust."
5. **Lineage erasure.** It reports a verdict without its evidence class — a Jev-advised probability stated as fact, a reflex-only "something moved" stated as a change. The ledger carries lineage; the spoken answer drops it.
6. **Cross-stream conflation.** It merges two streams' lines into one sentence ("the camera and the shelf both updated") because both appeared in context. The ledger has stream ids; the sentence does not.
7. **Fabricated specificity.** Numbers, times, names invented to fill the sentence shape — "three people at the door" from a tile count. Small models do this under grounding pressure.
8. **Refusal-to-answer collapse.** The opposite failure: it refuses a question the ledger can answer because the retrieved context is ambiguous, which trains the user to stop asking.

## (b) Cheapest architecture, ranked by cost-to-trust

**Rank 1 — Verdict-only grounding (the brain may only verbalise structured verdicts).** The brain never sees the ledger. It sees a *pre-selected* set of verdict records — the same published verdicts the change feed consumes, each already carrying stream id, tick, evidence class, and (where sampled) Jev probability. Its job is one sentence per verdict, in a fixed grammar. This kills failure modes 1, 2, 5, 6, 7 outright: there is nothing to mis-select because selection already happened in the perception plane, and nothing to over-claim because the record's fields are the sentence's slots. Cost: near-zero (a template + a small model for fluency). Trust: high, because the failure surface collapses to paraphrase drift (mode 3), which is bounded by the record's own vocabulary. **This is the fix the observed failure is asking for.** The 1.2B model cherry-picked because it was allowed to cherry-pick.

**Rank 2 — Retrieval discipline: a deterministic selector owns ledger access.** If the brain must answer open questions ("what did I miss?"), a small non-LLM selector — a query planner over the ledger's known schema (per-stream latest verdict, feed-since-timestamp, stream health) — picks the lines and hands the brain a *closed* context with citations. The brain cites by id; the renderer resolves ids to text. Cost: one afternoon of schema-aware code, no model. Trust: high for the questions the selector covers; the selector's coverage is the honest limit, and uncovered questions get "I can't answer that from what I watch" (mode 4 handled correctly). This is the right *second* layer, and it subsumes Rank 1 for the open-question path.

**Rank 3 — Escalation to a bigger brain, triggered and budgeted.** Trigger on: (i) the small brain's answer fails a self-check (cites an id not in context, or emits a number not in any record — cheap string check), (ii) the question is classified "open" by the selector and the selector's confidence is low, (iii) the user explicitly asks "why" or "explain." Escalate to the Thor's llama-server at the largest model that fits, or the hosted judge if the household permits egress (§2.7). Latency: seconds, acceptable for a follow-up question, not for the first sentence. Cost: bounded by trigger rate; log every escalation as a ledger event so the rate is a measured number, not a guess. Trust: medium — a bigger brain is still a free-reader unless Rank 1/2 discipline is applied to it too; escalation without grounding discipline just moves the failure up a weight class.

**Rank 4 — Confidence-gated speech.** The brain's sentence carries a confidence derived from the verdict's own evidence class (reflex-only < decision < refined < Jev-advised), and the voice says it ("something moved at the dock — I haven't perceived it closely"). Cost: trivial. Trust: medium; it makes mode 3 and mode 5 honest rather than fixing them.

**Rank 5 — Learn from corrections.** The "that's not what I asked" / "that's not a change" taps (§2.5) feed a small selector-tuning loop. Cost: real (a labelling pipeline). Trust: high but slow; this is a Slice-N+1 investment, not a first fix.

**The cheapest architecture that actually fixes the observed failure** is Rank 1 + Rank 2: verdict-only grounding for the fast path, a deterministic selector for open questions, escalation (Rank 3) only on the selector's named misses. The 1.2B model stays — it is a *verbaliser*, not a reader. The failure was architectural: the spec gave the small brain the ledger and asked it to be a retrieval system. Take the ledger away.
